The first time I ever heard of a protocol-based social network was in a discussion about hypothetical anti-trust action taken against Facebook. We could picture Facebook being split into two different platforms, as had been done with AT&T in 1982, but it's hard to imagine it working with a social network. I would want to be on the same baby-Facebook as my friends, who would want to join their friends, and before long we'd be right back at there being only one de-facto Facebook again. We could also imagine a judgement that such a product as Facebook could never be anything but a consumer-hostile monopoly and that it should be nationalized and operated as a public good, similar (from a legal perspective) to how NPR works, but we live in a post-Reagan America so anyone proposing such an idea would be run out of congress as a communist agent. The solution that was proposed to me at the time was to abolish Facebook as a platform and instead reimagine it as a shared form of communication that could be distributed across many small platforms: a protocol. Schools, local governments, companies, and even particularly enterprising individuals could host their own Facebooks, with the shared protocols allowing them to interact as seamlessly as the centralized platform, but with no overarching owner to control it.

Although this idea isn't any more likely to be put into practice under the Sherman Antitrust Act than the previous ideas, it has the benefit of being actionable. It has, in fact, been acted on. Mastodon and Bluesky both operate on a similar principle, although their model is more similar to Twitter than to Facebook. This gives us a look at how this concept can work in practice. They certainly haven't developed the same market penetration that their centralized alternative has, but we at least can see this as achievable from a technical perspective.

So why would I throw my own hat in this ring? Well the first time I tried it it was just an excuse to refresh myself on Spring Boot, a technology I hadn't touched in the better part of a decade, when I was laid off from the company that made Micronaut. Now that my day job is almost exclusively Spring Boot I wanted to revisit the idea in Micronaut and keep that skill sharp as well.

One thing I'm doing a bit different than the existing distributed social networks, at least to the best of my knowledge, is maintaining a degree of extensibility to individual nodes. My focus in this very early stage is features that would be universal such as user management and administration with virtually every actual user feature being an optional plugin that could be added on. Do you want your node on this social network to feature blogging? That's a plugin. Did you want to upload images to display in your blogs? Better provide a file hosting plugin too. Of course I have to call myself out a bit on this idea as well. Making a tool that can do anything is easy. That's a compiler, and every computer science student makes one in a 300-level class. Making a tool that can do anything and then doing anything useful with it will be the real challenge.

I want to close on speculating about use-cases for a tool like this. There are plenty of complaints about the existing social networks. Algorithmic feeds that leads to addiction. The ease with which misinformation spreads. That time Facebook maybe caused a genocide in Myanmar. Knowing that these distributed networks are unlikely to actually displace the established social networks means focusing on ending the harm they cause isn't productive. It's better to look at the uses people might find outside of those existing social networks. We can imagine, for example, small isolated nodes for people who just want to keep in touch with friends but don't want the baggage of being on the larger social networks. We can look at the ability for niche tools or even video games that can be integrated directly into a social network. A frequent complaint among online sex workers is that they are driven off of social networks (in 2021 OnlyFans threatened to ban them, and I thought until then that was the point of that site), a problem that could possibly be alleviate by having a social network under their own control. One might even imagine labor organizers being able to collect union support without workers having to reveal the intent and risk retaliation.

But these are very pie-in-the-sky ideas and this 0.1 release is a long way from anything like that.

The update to the browser client is done, and looks prettier but is almost entirely functionally unchanged. I removed the chronological view of the work history section because it was not sufficiently distinct from the categorical view. I have some ideas for bringing it back, but that's a much heavier lift than I wanted to include in this update. The same general sections exist, but they're all collapsible. I have added a loading spinner.

The loading spinner is to cover for hosting. I'm not expecting a lot of traffic to this site, so the goal is to not keep the machines running full time. I'm just too cheap to have everything load immediately, and I'm sorry for that. I have added a minimum of one machine always on for the angular projects, so at least the loading spinner should load up almost immediately.

I'm not entirely satisfied with some of the changes I've made from a technical perspective. I'm worried I've developed something of a cargo cult mentality with respect to the Angular-Material themes, for example. The theme file I'm using comes from a theme generator, and I don't think I could make it myself. Ideally I should be able to swap the theme file out with another one and I would just get different fonts and colors, all across the file, but I haven't tested this and I'm not confident it would work. That said, I'm not beating myself up over this too much since AngularJS mastery isn't really the skill I'm looking to demonstrate with this portfolio

While working on the update to the interactive resume I showed it to my roommate. I was hoping for some input on the appearance, because I don't have a lot of confidence in my ability to make a UI pleasing to the eye. It was her first time seeing the website at all, probably the first time anyone but me had looked at it, in fact. She laughed at it, at the idea that my developer portfolio would contain a blog section, and at the logo, the curly-brace abacus.

I'll start by confessing I don't know how to use an abacus, so even when I say "I don't believe the abacus in my logo could function" I can't really say that for certain. My understanding is it should have multiple layers of beads, allowing representation of larger values the same way having multiple digits works in arabic numerals, but I googled "abacus" and I guess even that isn't necessarily true. If nothing else I feel comfortable saying that the curly brace model would make the abacus very uncomfortable to hold.

So why, if I don't understand or care to learn about the nuances of this tool, would I spend the better part of 20 minutes in GIMP making this logo? The abacus is not an important tool in modern day computing. My logo is not a good abacus. The logo is evocative of an abacus, and the abacus is evocative of computing as a concept, serving, as it did, as the ur computer. When I was in the Air Force I served in a software development unit that used a wizard holding an abacus as its mascot, so I'm not even being particularly original here.

This is all probably a bit more defensive than I need to be about a silly logo, but I wanted to have something to put out while I struggle to pry myself away from Rimworld long enough to finish the interactive resume update.

The time span between the last post and this one is how long it took me to get from coding this simple Angular app to finding a hosting service I was happy with. I tried Google Cloud first, but when I found myself staring down the barrel of a $200 bill after trying to follow a few tutorials I decided that might not be the way to go. I tried Cloudbees briefly, and I think if I ever find myself in need of serious CI/CD I'll come back to that, but it was too involved for my very simple needs. The hosting service I ended up using, fly.io, is more to my speed. I do a lot of my local development in Docker containers, so a hosting service that uses dockerfiles to define the production deployment makes the transition from dev to prod very straightforward.

So that's the site up and running, but I can't very well call it quits there. The most immediate problem is fixes to the interactive resume. Since it was my first time building an AngularJS project from the ground up I had chosen to use native Angular as much as possible, as compared to this home page, which uses Angular's material-ui library. That's why this looks so much nicer, and why if you try to pull up the interactive resume on your phone you'll get a completely un-scaled, un-optimized travesty, whereas this home page is even smart enough to hide certain elements on a small screen. For that reason the next code update is going to be a re-do of the interactive resume's UI component from the ground up with material-ui to allow scaling for mobile and a more consistent look with this home page.
